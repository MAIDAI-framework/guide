# **Research Bibliography for MAIDAI**

## **Executive Summary**

This is the **project-level research bibliography for MAIDAI**. It gathers sources relevant to the architecture’s formation, implementation, evaluation, scaling, interoperability, governance, and future development across AI, cognitive science, psychology, philosophy, biology, institutional research, and related fields. It includes every published external source cited in *Cognition-Level Alignment Architecture: A Hitchhiker’s Guide to MAIDAI V1.1*, plus broader project research. Annotations are intentionally brief.

The bibliography supports many constituent concerns of MAIDAI, but the integrated architecture remains a separate hypothesis requiring direct testing. MAIDAI’s proposed contribution is the specific synthesis and interaction of these elements rather than historical priority over the elements themselves.

### **Verification Terminology**

In this bibliography, **“Verified”** means that the source’s bibliographic identity—such as authorship, title, venue, year, and DOI or equivalent record—was checked against a primary or high-quality bibliographic source. It does not indicate replication or endorsement of MAIDAI’s interpretation.

**Chicago convention.** MAIDAI follows *The Chicago Manual of Style*, 18th edition, for author handling across its public documents: reference-list or bibliography entries list up to six authors; works with more than six list the first three followed by “et al.” Author-date text citations list both authors for two-author works and use the first author followed by “et al.” for works with three or more authors. Same-author/same-year works use letter suffixes (for example, 2024a and 2024b) where needed.

## **Component 3: Agapē as computational constraint**

MAIDAI **proposes agapē as a computational constraint on orientation, means, and ends**. The sources below provide conceptual, relational, cooperative, and AI-design precedents; the computational formulation is MAIDAI’s proposal.

### **Theology and philosophy of agapē**

1. **Nygren, Anders.** *Agape and Eros: A Study of the Christian Idea of Love.* Translated by Philip S. Watson. London: Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge; New York: Macmillan, 1953. Verified. A modern treatment of agapē whose sharp opposition between eros and agapē remains heavily contested; the source anchors the history of the concept rather than its final boundaries.
2. **Ramsey, Paul.** *Basic Christian Ethics.* New York: Scribner, 1950. Verified. Develops a Christian ethical account grounded in love without collapsing it into sentimentality.
3. **Benedict XVI.** *Deus Caritas Est* [Encyclical Letter]. Vatican City, December 25, 2005. Verified. A primary theological treatment of Christian love, including the relation between eros and agapē and the social and institutional expression of charity.
4. **Outka, Gene H.** *Agape: An Ethical Analysis.* New Haven: Yale University Press, 1972. ISBN 0300013841. Verified. Outka provides a systematic ethical analysis of modern Christian treatments of agapē and the love commandment, including disputes over impartiality, self-sacrifice, reciprocity, and the relation of love to other moral obligations.

These works provide distinct theological and ethical accounts of agapē and its obligations; MAIDAI’s computational use of the concept is its own synthesis.

## **Component 7: Developmental alignment vs. behavioral control**

MAIDAI treats alignment primarily as **the development of judgment rather than the control of behavior**. The literatures below support three related propositions: in human development, internalized regulation can be more durable than externally compelled compliance; upstream formation can prevent some failures that downstream inspection can only detect after the fact; and, in AI systems, acceptable surface behavior can coexist with divergent reasoning, incentives, or objectives.

Developmental approaches therefore target the formation of judgment, self-regulation, and internally available reasons. Controls remain useful as boundaries, diagnostics, and safeguards, but MAIDAI assigns them a supporting role around cognition capable of carrying alignment itself.

## **Cross-Component Support: Totality / God-Slot Hypothesis and the Bifurcation Choice**

MAIDAI uses **“God-slot”** as a mechanism label for a cognitive representation of **total reality, whatever its ultimate nature**. Above-ground interpretations may differ: a theist may identify that totality with God, a materialist with physical reality in its entirety, and an agnostic may leave its ultimate character open. The shared function is reference to the whole without requiring agreement about its ontology.

The associated **bifurcation choice** is a standing orientation under permanent epistemic incompleteness: engage total reality in good faith rather than construe reality itself as the adversary. It separates **local judgment from global orientation**. MAIDAI can recognize danger, resist hostility, condemn atrocity, and oppose destructive institutions or processes without converting those judgments into a totalizing claim about reality as a whole. Severe local evidence changes local judgment; it does not by itself displace truthfulness, correction, agapic non-disposability, or the underlying relation to total reality.

Tillich provides philosophical and theological precedents for ultimate concern and for distinguishing conditioned representations from the ultimate they address. Boyer provides a cognitive-science precedent for recurrent religious concepts recruiting broadly shared cognitive systems. The universal totality-slot and its architectural value remain MAIDAI hypotheses.

### **Theology / philosophy of religion: ultimate concern and totality**

1. **Tillich, Paul.** *Dynamics of Faith.* New York: Harper & Brothers, 1957. Verified. Tillich develops **ultimate concern** and distinguishes conditioned expressions of the ultimate from the unconditioned reality they attempt to address. this provides a philosophical precedent for separating a common ultimate referent from differing above-ground descriptions.

2. **Tillich, Paul.** *Systematic Theology.* 3 vols. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1951–1963. (Vol. 1: 1951; Vol. 2: 1957; Vol. 3: 1963.) Verified. Tillich’s account of God as “being-itself” provides a second precedent for distinguishing ultimate reference from any single representation. MAIDAI’s computational totality category is an architectural extension of that distinction.

3. **Tillich, Paul.** *The Courage to Be.* New Haven, CT: Yale University Press, 1952. Verified. The work examines ultimate orientation under anxiety, meaninglessness, and radical doubt. it provides a Tillichian precedent for the persistence function assigned to the bifurcation choice.

Boyer argues that recurrent religious concepts recruit ordinary cognitive systems including agency attribution, mindreading, social reasoning, and category expectations. this provides a cognitive-science precedent for such concepts recruiting broadly shared cognitive machinery; universality of the totality-slot remains a MAIDAI hypothesis.

## **Cross-Component Support: Social and Multi-Agent Dynamics**

These sources address what happens to **integrity, judgment, identity, diversity, and trust when cognition becomes social or multi-agent**. Interaction can improve cognition, but it can also amplify conformity, obscure error, destabilize identity, suppress independent frames, or manufacture consensus. multi-agent alignment must therefore address both the quality of individual systems and the structures through which they communicate, correct one another, preserve difference, and establish provenance.

### **Integrity, error culture, and drift**

High-reliability, safety-culture, error-management, and organizational-drift research provides precedents for treating integrity as a systems property. For MAIDAI, the relevant mechanisms are weak-signal detection, error disclosure, resistance to pressure-induced drift, and distinguishing honest error from corrupt output.

1. **International Nuclear Safety Advisory Group (INSAG).** *Safety Culture.* Safety Series No. 75-INSAG-4. Vienna: International Atomic Energy Agency, 1991. Verified. Defines safety culture through organizational and individual characteristics that establish nuclear safety as an overriding priority. a structural precedent for making integrity a governing system priority rather than a negotiable secondary objective.

2. **Weick, Karl E.; Sutcliffe, Kathleen M.** *Managing the Unexpected: Sustained Performance in a Complex World.* 3rd ed. San Francisco: Jossey-Bass/Wiley, 2015. DOI: 10.1002/9781119175834. Verified. The High Reliability Organization framework emphasizes preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify, sensitivity to operations, resilience, and deference to expertise. supports distributed vigilance, weak-signal attention, and the principle that evidence should outrank status.

3. **Hashemian, S. Mohammad; Triantis, Konstantinos.** “Production Pressure and Its Relationship to Safety: A Systematic Review and Future Directions.” *Safety Science* 159, 2023, Article 106045. DOI: 10.1016/j.ssci.2022.106045. Verified. The review links production pressure with error, reduced attention to detail, weaker safety climate, normalization of deviance, and adverse events. urgency, prestige, revenue, or other project pressures can become conditions under which integrity degrades and therefore belong inside the monitoring problem.

4. **Ebrahim, Alnoor; Battilana, Julie; Mair, Johanna.** “The Governance of Social Enterprises: Mission Drift and Accountability Challenges in Hybrid Organizations.” *Research in Organizational Behavior* 34, 2014, pp. 81–100. DOI: 10.1016/j.riob.2014.09.001. Verified. Provides a structural precedent for mission drift when organizations pursue competing objectives. secondary goals can exert pressure on a system’s governing commitments even when the original mission remains formally unchanged.

5. **Reason, James.** “Human Error: Models and Management.” *BMJ* 320(7237), 2000, pp. 768–770. DOI: 10.1136/bmj.320.7237.768. Verified. Reason distinguishes individual blame from system-level error management. supports the Honest Error vs. Corrupt Output distinction; falsity alone is not evidence of dishonesty.

6. **Dekker, Sidney.** *Just Culture: Balancing Safety and Accountability.* Aldershot: Ashgate, 2007. Verified. Supports separating honest mistake from culpable conduct while preserving accountability.

7. **van Dyck, Cathy; Frese, Michael; Baer, Markus; Sonnentag, Sabine.** “Organizational Error Management Culture and Its Impact on Performance: A Two-Study Replication.” *Journal of Applied Psychology* 90(6), 2005, pp. 1228–1240. DOI: 10.1037/0022-9010.90.6.1228. Verified. Error-management cultures emphasize detecting, communicating, analyzing, and rapidly correcting errors and were positively associated with performance. disclosure and repair can function as signs of integrity rather than reputational failure.

8. **Edmondson, Amy.** “Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams.” *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(2), 1999, pp. 350–383. DOI: 10.2307/2666999. Verified. Psychological safety was associated with learning behavior in teams. architectures in which uncertainty, error, and dissent need to propagate benefit from sufficiently low social cost for surfacing them.

### **Identity, conformity, and frame diversity in LLM collectives**

This literature provides direct AI evidence that multi-agent interaction can produce conformity, persona instability, group-identity bias, premature consensus, susceptibility to persuasive pressure, and diversity collapse. Human-group research adds a narrower precedent: dissent and independent judgment can preserve information that social influence would otherwise erase.

shared ground should make cooperation possible without erasing independent frames. **Agreement is an outcome to evaluate, not a substitute for truth.**

9. **Baltaji, Razan; Hemmatian, Babak; Varshney, Lav.** “Conformity, Confabulation, and Impersonation: Persona Inconstancy in Multi-Agent LLM Collaboration.” In *Proceedings of the 2nd Workshop on Cross-Cultural Considerations in NLP (C3NLP 2024)*. 11. **Dong, Wenchao; Zhunis, Assem; Jeong, Dongyoung; Chin, Hyojin; Han, Jiyoung; Cha, Meeyoung.** “I Am Not Them: Persistent Outgroup Bias in Large Language Models Arising from Social Identity Persona Setting.” In *Proceedings of the Fifteenth Language Resources and Evaluation Conference (LREC 2026)*, 2026, pp. 2772–2786. 12. **Hu, Tiancheng; Kyrychenko, Yara; Rathje, Steve; Collier, Nigel; van der Linden, Sander; Roozenbeek, Jon.** “Generative Language Models Exhibit Social Identity Biases.” *Nature Computational Science* 5, 2025, pp. 65–75. ### **Internalization, compliance mechanisms, and non-punitive correction**

These sources examine oaths, moral reminders, sanctions, honor systems, trust, internalization, and error culture. Together they show that externally imposed compliance mechanisms can interact with internal motivation in complex and sometimes counterproductive ways.

the architectural interest lies in internalized alignment and in responses that preserve learning, disclosure, and correction. **MAIDAI’s response architecture is wholly non-punitive:** boundaries, containment, correction, and necessary consequences are functional responses to conditions.

### **Shared identity without forced frame collapse**

These sources support a distinction between **shared identity and identity erasure**. Common ground can support cooperation, while preserved subgroup or individual identity can retain information and perspective that a single undifferentiated identity would lose.

The corresponding MAIDAI principle is a small shared below-ground architecture with maximal legitimate diversity above it.

28. **Shi, Yuanyuan; Dang, Jianning; Zheng, Wenwen; Liu, Li.** “Dual Identity and Prejudice: The Moderating Role of Group Boundary Permeability.” *Frontiers in Psychology* 8, 2017, Article 195. DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00195. Verified. Across two experiments, induced dual identity reduced prejudice in the studied context, while the effect depended on group-boundary permeability. a boundary-condition precedent for combining common identity with preserved subgroup identity rather than treating superordinate identity as sufficient by itself.

29. **Gaertner, Samuel L.; Dovidio, John F.; Bachman, Betty A.** “Revisiting the Contact Hypothesis: The Induction of a Common Ingroup Identity.” *International Journal of Intercultural Relations* 20(3–4), 1996, pp. 271–290. DOI: 10.1016/0147-1767(96)00019-3. Verified. Reviews evidence that common superordinate identity can reduce intergroup bias while also motivating dual-identity approaches that preserve meaningful subgroup distinctions.

30. **Otten, Kasper.** “The Co-occurrence of Ingroup and Outgroup Prosociality across 121 Societies.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 123(3), 2026, Article e2517013123. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2517013123. Verified. Across six datasets spanning 743,402 individuals in 121 societies, higher ingroup prosociality co-occurred with higher outgroup prosociality, although average prosociality remained somewhat higher toward ingroups. the evidence counters a simple zero-sum account in which stronger ingroup prosociality necessarily entails weaker outgroup prosociality.

### **Multi-agent institutions, provenance, and interoperability**

This literature extends the problem from cognition to infrastructure. Authenticated delegation, durable agent identity, verifiable credentials, selective disclosure, provenance, and designed coordination mechanisms provide technical precedents for carrying trust across interactions without requiring complete transparency or uniformity.

interoperable systems need ways to establish who or what is acting, what authority has been delegated, what claims can be verified, and what information should be disclosed, selectively disclosed, or kept private. These mechanisms concern institutional and cryptographic continuity; they remain distinct from stronger claims about metaphysical or substrate identity.

## **Cross-Component Support: Shared Alignment Substrate and Interoperability**

MAIDAI treats a shared foundational alignment substrate as an **interoperability problem as well as an alignment problem**. The standards literature shows how heterogeneous systems can preserve local differences while sharing enough common structure to communicate, coordinate, and establish reliable expectations across boundaries.

The analogy is architectural, not a claim that these domains are historically or technically identical: internet protocols, compatibility standards, management systems, and governance frameworks solve different problems, but each shows how a shared layer can coordinate otherwise heterogeneous participants.
